MEng Dissertation · University of Sheffield · 2025

Understanding and Simulating Flows Through VR

I simulated transonic airflow over the ONERA M6 wing in ANSYS Fluent, validated the results against wind tunnel data, and built an interactive VR environment in Unity for exploring them: walking around the wing, sliding through pressure planes, watching the flow animate and probing velocities with a controller.

The problem

CFD results are usually viewed as 2D graphs and static contours in menu-heavy software, which makes 3D phenomena like shockwaves and flow separation hard to picture. The project asked whether VR could make them intuitive, using the ONERA M6 transonic benchmark, which produces a clear shockwave and shock-induced separation.

Pipeline: from solver to headset

  1. ANSYS FluentSteady RANS, exported as EnSight Gold
  2. ParaView / CFD-PostPlanes & surfaces as X3D, streamlines as CSV
  3. BlenderVertex colours → FBX
  4. Unity 6Custom shader, C# particles, XR UI
  5. Meta Quest 2Quest Link / Air Link

CFD setup

I built a hemispherical domain ten root chords in diameter around the swept ONERA M6 wing, with a refined body of influence over the wake and tip vortex. I used a tetrahedral mesh with local sizing down to 3.75 mm at the root and tip. To use Spalart–Allmaras wall functions I targeted y+ ≈ 30. Working from Sutherland's law, the freestream conditions and a skin-friction estimate (uτ = 10.40 m/s), I calculated a first inflation layer height of 4.64×10−5 m. The pressure-based solver modelled air as an ideal gas at Mach 0.84 for two test cases: α = 3.06° (mostly attached flow) and 6.06° (shock-induced separation).

Validation against experiment

In a four-level mesh study (0.89M to 7.35M cells), going from the fine to the ultra-fine mesh changed CL by less than 0.5%, so I used the fine mesh (3.48M cells) for all results.

At 3.06° the simulation closely matches experiment. At 6.06° it under-predicts lift and over-predicts drag, a known limitation of the Spalart–Allmaras model with shock-induced separation. The pressure coefficient (Cp) distributions show the same pattern. They agree closely wherever the flow is attached, but diverge outboard of about 65% span at 6.06°, where the flow separates.

Flow features

A strong leading-edge suction peak is followed by a shockwave at about 50–60% chord at the root, moving forward towards the tip. At 6.06° the shock strengthens and the flow begins to recirculate near the trailing edge.

The VR environment

The environment runs in Unity 6 with the XR Interaction Toolkit. I wrote the data handling and interaction systems in C#.

  • Grabbable contour planes: seven spanwise pressure and velocity planes that users slide from root to tip, rendered with a custom unlit vertex-colour shader so the colours match ParaView exactly.
  • Animated particle flow: streamline data from CSV drives a pooled particle system coloured by velocity. Particles race through the suction peak and linger in the separated region, which is hard to see in static plots but obvious in VR.
  • Velocity probe: point the controller at any particle to read its speed, for example 454 m/s at the suction peak, 186 m/s pre-shock and 20.7 m/s post-shock.
  • World-space menus: play, pause and restart the animation, switch angle of attack, and toggle contours.

Limitations & next steps

  • Performance: tens of thousands of particle objects ran at about 42 FPS, below the Quest 2's 72 Hz target.
  • Post-processing only: next steps would be linking the environment to a real-time solver or reduced-order model so users can change the angle of attack live.
  • Evaluation: run formal usability studies with students (System Usability Scale, NASA-TLX) to measure the learning benefit.
