Brick Flyer: VTOL Quadplane UAS

As Project Manager of a six-person team, I led the design, build, certification and flight of a vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) quadplane for a simulated industry tender: a search-and-location drone that takes off like a quadcopter, transitions to efficient fixed-wing flight, and lands itself.

The brief

In September 2023, AeroMAP, the module's simulated client, issued a tender for an advanced VTOL unmanned air system for search-and-location missions. The aim was to combine quadcopter-style vertical take-off with fixed-wing forward flight for more range, endurance and payload, and to fly parts of the mission autonomously. The statement of requirements set out:

  • Electric propulsion only, with 10 minutes of flight: vertical take-off, transition to fixed-wing flight, autonomous navigation to a rally point, and automated landing.
  • Must pack, disassembled, into a 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.2 m box, and be flight-ready within 10 minutes of arriving on site.
  • Maximum mass of 2 kg including battery, foamboard-and-spar wings, and a structure that can be disassembled for reuse.
  • Strict safety: automatic and manual shutdown, plus a GeoFence to keep the aircraft inside the mission area.

My role: Project Manager

Each team member owned one discipline: structures, flight dynamics, avionics flight systems, ground systems and mission planning, and integration. My job was to make those six work packages add up to one aircraft, on time and on budget:

  • Planning: owned the project plan, with every work package, owner and milestone (manufacture gate, testing gate, certification, flight day, final presentations) tracked week by week.
  • Stakeholder reporting: fortnightly "5-box" status reports to the client, covering milestone status against baseline, risks and mitigations, cost, issues and dependencies, and completed and planned work.
  • Documentation: responsible for the quality and on-time submission of all project documents: statement of work, project plan, risk register, RACI matrix, safety case and technical report. I was also the reviewer on the safety case and technical report.
  • Finance: owned the cost tracking and the final financial review of the project.

Turning the team around

The project started badly. With few meetings and patchy attendance, our first planning documents were poor, and the feedback was a wake-up call. We reset with scheduled weekly meetings with a set purpose, status tracking on a shared drive, and a properly rebuilt project plan. From then on the work stayed on schedule, document quality improved steadily through the year, and every slipped work package was recovered.

Design philosophy

We agreed three principles early and used them to settle design arguments quickly:

  • Simplicity: easy to design, manufacture and assemble. We took a known-good layout from our test rig and changed as little as possible.
  • Efficiency: minimise weight and flight speed, and maximise wing area, to stretch the flight time.
  • Robustness: a solid frame with impact-absorbing 3D-printed fixtures that can survive knocks and crashes.

Design & build

We followed the CADMID life cycle (Concept, Assessment, Demonstration, Manufacture, In-service, Disposal):

Concept: CAD design

The design centres on a compact "chassis" sized to fit the transport box, made of CFRP box-section booms joined by 3D-printed brackets. The VTOL motors sit at the ends of the booms, the forward-flight motor in the nose, and the wings and twin-fin tail detach for transport.

Assessment: analysis before cutting foam

  • FEA (Fusion 360): compared CFRP and aluminium box sections as cantilever beams. CFRP gave the lowest displacement for its weight, so we used it for the frame. Next we tested a simulated wing. Under a 30 N overload, three times the expected 10 N, the peak stress was 1.42 MPa, far below foamboard's 28 MPa strength.
  • XFoil: compared aerofoil shapes to guide a large, high-lift wing, accepting that a hand-built foamboard section can only approximate the ideal profile.

Demonstration: the iron bird

Before building the aircraft, we built an iron bird: a ground test rig carrying the full avionics in the final layout. It let us sort out wiring lengths, get the software working, spin the motors and test the servos early. It also caught a potentially catastrophic flaw: the planned motor positions would have made the propellers collide, so we moved the motors before building the real airframe. It also showed that the first frame was slightly too large for the transport box, and that the servo leads were too short for the chosen flight controller position.

In parallel, the structures team built foamboard wing prototypes. The first wing joint, screws in the foam tied together with wire, was too flimsy to fly, which led directly to the CFRP spar and dowel joint used on the final aircraft.

Manufacture

  • Early 3D printing: the 3D-printed brackets were designed and submitted to the print queue early, missing the long delays that followed, so frame construction could start straight away.
  • Stronger core: a design review found the first concept relied too heavily on the foamboard centre wing. We added a plywood board inside it to carry the spars, wings and avionics stack.
  • Wing joints: a load-bearing CFRP spar at about a third of the chord slots onto a wooden dowel, with a second, shorter tube to stop the wing twisting. Velcro straps hold it all in place, so assembly is fast and the wing is stiff.
  • Avionics integration: because the iron bird matched the final layout, integration was quick: components moved straight across in the same configuration. The flight electronics sit in a front stack, and the power electronics sit inside the wing, reached through a Velcro access strip.
  • Prop change: in the first net test the aircraft was too heavy to hover on 5″ props, so we upgraded to 6″ two-blade props. This also improved stability.

Safety & certification

Before flying, the team had to earn a Flight Test Certificate by submitting an Air System Safety Case, written using CAA guidance (CAP 722A), which I reviewed. The aircraft had several layers of protection:

  • A GeoFence around the test site, with automatic return to a rally point if it was breached or the signal was lost.
  • A transmitter kill switch, plus dual arming: both the pilot and the ground station operator must act to arm it.
  • A 30 m altitude limit and a low-battery failsafe that lands the aircraft automatically.
  • Insulated wiring, and motors mounted on 3D-printed parts to isolate them from the carbon-fibre booms.

Flight day

At the Derbyshire and Lancashire Gliding Club, Brick Flyer was the first team to launch. Two hover tests showed a stable take-off, manoeuvring and automatic landing, with no vibration or oscillation.

The crash and how we diagnosed it

The first forward flight transitioned cleanly, then ended in a nosedive after a mode change. We worked out why by lining up the on-board video with the flight logs in UAV Log Viewer:

  1. The pilot's switch selected Loiter instead of RTL, and the autopilot banked right automatically.
  2. The sideslip from that roll pushed the measured airspeed above the VTOL assist limit, so the lift motors shut off.
  3. Without that lift, the aircraft pitched up, stalled and dived into the ground.

We repaired it on site and flew again the same day. The second flight was the best of the project: a transition to forward flight, a climb, a full piloted circuit, then a transition back to hover and an automatic landing.

Budget

The team had £800. Most of it went on propulsion and avionics, and the remaining contingency covered the prop upgrade and extra materials. I tracked spending against estimates throughout and reported changes to the client in each 5-box.

The cost analysis split spending into mandatory components (flight controller, GPS, ESCs, telemetry and so on), optional components chosen from a range (motors, props, battery, servos) and materials, and tracked each one from the initial estimate to final expenditure. One trade-off was choosing the 3000 mAh battery over a larger one, partly on cost.

What I learned as Project Manager

  • Structure from day one: regular meetings with a clear purpose, and a plan the whole team believes in, are what keep a project on track. We learned that the hard way and recovered.
  • Plan time for prototypes and testing: the iron bird and early net testing found the prop clash, the underpowered props and the ground-effect issues while they were still cheap to fix. Bringing the testing gate forward to week 6 gave us two extra weeks to iterate.
  • Double-check the small things: a couple of wrong parameters delayed our Flight Test Certificate, and an unlabelled switch caused the crash. Labelling switches and running checklists cost almost nothing.
  • Stay calm and use the data: after the crash, diagnosing the cause from the logs instead of guessing meant we could fix it and fly again the same day.
